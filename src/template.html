Product names · place names · Britain, July 2022 to October 2026

Place Settings

Four in five IKEA rugs on sale in 2022 were named after a Danish town. Of the rugs launched since, one in six is.

Everybody knows how IKEA names things: beds after Norwegian places, rugs after Danish ones, sofas after Swedish towns, curtains after girls, desks after boys. The rulebook is repeated everywhere and checked almost nowhere. Below, every product name IKEA has sold in Britain since July 2022 (4,064 of them, read from the company's own sitemaps and the Internet Archive's copies of them) is put through a gazetteer, a population register and a dictionary, rule by rule. Most of the rules were real. Several are being quietly retired.

The rulebook, rule by rule

Fifteen claims, in the form they are usually repeated, tested on the names on sale today. A rule has to beat the background: the share of every other name in the range that happens to come from the same source. The p value asks how often a random set of names of the same size would score as high; it is recomputed in your browser from 2,000 shuffles.

The instrument

Each yes or no above comes from matching a name, letter for letter, against a list. Before any of it was trusted, every list was run over the 1,362 names in Lars Petrus's hand-labelled IKEA Dictionary (about 2010), where a person had already decided what each name is. That gives each instrument a catch rate on real places or people, and a false-alarm rate on ordinary words.

Norway has a place called everything

GeoNames knows 362,144 distinct Norwegian names, against 68,839 Swedish: Norway's national mapping agency fed in every farm, hill, bog and skerry. Matched against every feature, 10% of Petrus's ordinary Swedish words turn out to be somewhere in Norway (MALM, IKEA's best-known bed, is a Norwegian village; Petrus says it is the Swedish word for ore). Matched against populated places only, 2% are. Every place figure on this page uses populated places.

Methods

Sources

  • IKEA en-GB product sitemaps, ikea.com/sitemaps/prod-en-GB_1..6.xml, read 2026-10-13: 28,699 product URLs. Each URL slug carries the product name, an English description and the article number. Facts about what is on sale; no IKEA text or image is reproduced.
  • The same sitemaps as captured by the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine, July 2022 to October 2026, one capture of each file per quarter. The archive has nothing earlier: IKEA's product sitemaps were first captured in July 2022.
  • GeoNames country dumps for Sweden, Norway, Denmark and Finland (CC BY 4.0).
  • Wikidata: villages, hamlets, towns, cities, settlements, farms and Danish parishes in the four countries (CC0).
  • Statistics Sweden (SCB), first names people go by (tilltalsnamn), with at least two bearers, 31 December 2022 (open data, attribution).
  • SALDO, Språkbanken Text, University of Gothenburg: a lexicon of modern Swedish with a "kind of" link for each sense (CC BY 4.0).
  • The IKEA Dictionary by Lars Petrus, lar5.com/ikea, about 2010: 1,362 names hand-labelled as word, boy, girl or place. The site states no licence, so only each name's label is used here, never the definitions.
  • The rulebook as usually repeated: Quartz (2017), reprinted by Mental Floss and others; Wikipedia's IKEA article for the short form.

What was measured

  • One name, one category. A name's category is the commonest product type over the articles that carry it alone; names that only ever appear inside combinations use the articles they head. A door is part of what it hangs on: doors sold with the PAX frame are wardrobes, with BILLY bookcases, the rest kitchen fronts.
  • One spelling. IKEA's URLs fold Ä to AE, Ö to OE and Å to A. Every list was folded the same way, so a match is a match in one shared spelling. Å cannot be put back, so a name no source recognised is shown with a reconstructed spelling, marked with an asterisk in the search.
  • Cohorts. A name is "2022" if it was on sale at any point from July to December 2022, otherwise it belongs to the year it first appears. Taking all of 2022 as the baseline stops a sitemap file the archive happened to miss in July from making an old name look new in October.
  • Two measures, never averaged. Measure A is a place gazetteer or the name register: is this name a place or a person? Measure B is the dictionary: is it an ordinary Swedish word? They fail in different ways and are reported side by side.

The control, and what it showed

    What this does not say

    • It does not say when IKEA changed policy, or that anyone decided to. It says the names launched since 2023 follow some rules much less often than the names on sale in 2022. A rule can fade because the products that carried it were discontinued and their replacements were named by a different team, with no decision at all.
    • It does not date the 2022 names. Those are a floor: any of them could be forty years old.
    • It covers Britain only. IKEA uses the same names everywhere, but not every product is sold everywhere.
    • A name that matches no list is not proven to be anything. "None of these" includes coinages, compounds the dictionary lacks, species names and places too small for any list.
    • The categories are this page's, inferred from English product descriptions, not IKEA's own.

    What failed

    • The 2010 dictionary as a time axis. It looked like a free snapshot of 2010. It is not a sample: Petrus wrote down the names he could explain, so his names are far more often recognisable words and places than IKEA's were. Compared with today's range it shows a decline that is partly his selection. It is used only as a per-name second opinion and a calibration set.
    • Splitting compounds. The obvious fix for the dictionary missing compound words (HUSSPARV, house sparrow) is to accept any name that ends in a known noun. On Petrus's labels that rule calls 92% of words words, but also 51% of real places, because Scandinavian place names are compounds too: BERGSVIKEN is "the mountain's bay". It was dropped. The cost is that measure B is silent on curtains, beds and lighting, whose new names are mostly compounds it cannot see.
    • Denmark's own place-name service. Danmarks Adressers Web API, the first choice for a Danish second opinion, answered every request with HTTP 410 Gone. It has been retired. Wikidata stands in.
    • Wikidata for Sweden. It catches only 20% of Petrus's Swedish places (against 95% of his Danish ones), so it cannot check the Swedish rules. It is shown, not used, for those.
    • Archived category pages. The Wayback Machine has IKEA's rug listing pages from 2019 on, but those pages load their products by script and a capture holds only the first screenful. The sitemaps were used instead; they list everything but start in 2022.
